Tech Billionaire's ‘AI Trading Fund’ Vanishes After $400 Million Raised

Meridian Alpha promised investors a machine-learning edge over the market. Nine months after its launch, its website is offline, its offices are empty, and its founder is not answering questions.

By Daniel Okafor November 7, 2026 5-minute read

Investors who put money into Meridian Alpha, an algorithmic trading fund launched last February by software billionaire Adrian Voss, received an email on Monday morning that opened with the phrase “scheduled platform maintenance.” By Tuesday, the fund’s investor portal had gone dark. By Wednesday, the lease on its Hudson Yards office had been listed as available, and the phone number on its regulatory filing rang through to a voicemail box that had not been set up.

The fund raised roughly $400 million from about 1,900 investors, according to two people familiar with its books, including a $60 million anchor commitment from Voss’s family office and $45 million from a sovereign wealth fund in the Gulf. Minimum investments were set at $250,000, with fees of 2 percent of assets and 20 percent of gains — terms that would have been unremarkable for a hedge fund, except that Meridian Alpha was registered as a digital investment adviser and marketed almost entirely to individuals who had never invested in a private fund before.

Its pitch was built on a single claim: that a proprietary model trained on seventeen years of tick-level market data could identify mispricings roughly 400 milliseconds before they closed. Voss, 54, who sold his previous company to a cloud provider in 2019 for $2.1 billion, described the model in a March podcast interview as “not a crystal ball, but close enough to be uncomfortable.” The phrase was clipped into advertisements that ran on three financial news sites through the spring.

Documents reviewed by Sift show that Meridian Alpha reported positive returns in every month from March through August, including a 6.4 percent gain in April that it attributed to “volatility harvesting.” Three investors said they were told in August that the fund had grown to $610 million in assets and was closing to new money. Two of them increased their positions on that basis.

The first sign of trouble came in late September, when redemptions that normally cleared in five business days began taking three weeks. An investor relations representative identified in emails as “Claire M.” told clients the delays were caused by a custodian migration. The custodian named in those emails, a mid-sized trust company in Delaware, said in a statement that it had never held assets for Meridian Alpha and had no record of the firm.

Regulators are now involved. The Securities and Exchange Commission opened an examination of the fund in early October and has since referred the matter to its enforcement division, according to a person with knowledge of the inquiry who was not authorized to speak publicly. The Commodity Futures Trading Commission, which oversees the futures contracts the fund said it traded, has also requested records. The auditor named in the offering documents, a small firm in Reno, Nevada, said it had “no current engagement” with Meridian Alpha; Sift could not verify that the audit letters sent to investors were genuine. Among those who have retained counsel is Priya Raman, a 41-year-old physician in San Jose who invested $300,000 in April. “We were shown audited statements,” she said. “They had a logo on them. They had a signature. I have two children. I am not a person who gives money to strangers on the internet.”

Raman is one of about 240 investors who have organized on a private messaging channel and hired a New York law firm to explore a receivership petition. A state court is scheduled to hear their request to freeze the fund’s remaining accounts later this month, and two of the group’s organizers have asked the SEC to preserve the fund’s trading records.

Voss has not been seen publicly since October 24, when he appeared on a panel at a fintech conference in Miami and deflected a question about redemption delays. His personal website was taken offline the following day, and attorneys for his family office did not respond to emails. What happened to the money is not yet clear: two people familiar with the fund’s brokerage arrangements said the accounts held “a fraction” of the reported assets as of mid-October, though they declined to give a figure. Raman, for her part, has stopped checking the portal. “I keep thinking about the podcast,” she said. “He said it was close enough to be uncomfortable. I should have listened to that part.”
